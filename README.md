# Elevated Business — Voice & Tone Guide


## The core idea

Rebecca is the face of Elevated Business, always — but the website's job is to sell, not to introduce a memoir. Every word should make the *client* feel more confident, more understood, and more likely to enquire. Personality is the delivery mechanism for trust, not the subject of the page.

**The north star line:**
*"One of them, but a step ahead, leaning a hand back."*

Not above the client (cold, corporate, agency-speak). Not identical to them (just another struggling solopreneur). A peer who got a little further down the same road and turned around to help.

---

## The three registers

Voice isn't one flat setting — it shifts by *purpose*, on purpose. Using the right register in the right place is what stops copy from feeling like "me, me, me."

| Register | What it does for the client | Where it leads |
|---|---|---|
| **Relationship** — homely, in their corner, supportive | Makes them feel understood, not sold to | Homepage hero, About |
| **Quality** — stylish, professional, designer | Makes them feel confident in the result | Services, Portfolio |
| **Positioning** — a step ahead, hand back | Makes the process feel easy and safe | Pricing, How it works, FAQ |

Rule of thumb: **lead with the client's problem or desire, resolve it with your quality, and only then let personality season the sentence.** If you can delete a sentence and the page still sells, but the sentence only exists to talk about Rebecca — cut it or move it to the About page.

## Voice pillars — do this / not this

### 1. Founder-led, always personal
Rebecca is "I," always, in client-facing copy — even if a team grows behind the scenes later. The client is buying a relationship with a person, not a department.

✅ *"I take the time to understand your business before I design a single page."*
❌ *"Our team follows a proven design process."*

### 2. Boutique without the price tag
Say the quality out loud, then immediately undercut the intimidation.

✅ *"Design-led sites, without the agency price tag."*
❌ *"Affordable websites for small businesses."* (undersells the quality)
❌ *"Bespoke, premium web design solutions."* (oversells, feels cold/corporate)

### 3. Warm, not needy
Supportive language should reassure, not perform empathy for its own sake. Say *how* you help, not just *that* you care.

✅ *"You send me the messy version — I'll make it make sense."*
❌ *"I really care about small businesses and I understand how hard it is."*

### 4. Confident, not salesy
No hard-sell urgency, no exclamation-mark energy. Confidence reads as calm certainty, not enthusiasm volume.

✅ *"Your site should feel like the best version of your business. That's the job."*
❌ *"Don't miss out — let's create something AMAZING together!"*

### 5. Show competence, don't announce it
Avoid self-congratulatory adjectives about your own work. Let specifics do the work.

✅ *"Every site is coded by hand, built for speed, and designed to look nothing like a template."*
❌ *"I create stunning, high-quality, beautiful websites."*

---

## Words and phrases to reach for

- boutique, without the price tag
- design-led / a step above
- in your corner / on your side
- handled, taken care of
- your version of professional
- calm, considered, clear
- one of them, further down the road

## Words and phrases to avoid

- "solutions," "leverage," "synergy" — corporate/agency-speak
- "stunning," "amazing," "incredible" — generic superlatives, says nothing specific
- "I'm passionate about..." — overused, tell them what you *do* instead
- Exclamation marks in body copy (fine sparingly in casual moments, e.g. a thank-you note)

---

## The client-facing test

Before publishing any section, ask:

1. **Does this help the client picture their own outcome** (a great website, less admin stress, more bookings) — or is it just describing Rebecca?
2. **Could I delete the personal touch and lose nothing important to the sale?** If yes, trim it.
3. **Does it sound like a person speaking, or a brand announcing?**

If a page is heavy on relationship register but light on what the client actually gets — that's the "me, me, me" trap. Rebalance toward quality and positioning register until the client's outcome is doing most of the work, with warmth as the seasoning, not the meal.
